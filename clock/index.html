Life Unit World Clock

Life is the way it can be measured in units. Life Units.

One Life Unit (LU) is one day in the life every human deserves, costing about USD 2,692 today across the ten most expensive places to live. That dollar figure is a working placeholder until real prices are collected in each place. A Life Unit is not for sale; it is only a standard of measure.

Every number below moves continuously from the rates we measured or assumed: semi-real time, not live trading. Each number carries a tag that says how solid it is.

Official and instant

The daily fixed figure and the live feed

DEMONSTRATION, not a real rate. No official Life Unit figure has been released yet. Every dollar amount in this section is the working placeholder figure plus a simulated currency swing (the same for every viewer). It is here only to show how the official figure and the two feeds will work together.

When the standard is live, contracts will use only the official Daily Fixed figure: published once a day for everyone at the same moment, averaged over the 15:00 to 16:00 UTC window so nobody can push it at a single instant, and shown with the times of the observations behind it. Paying subscribers never receive it early. Businesses will be able to pay for the instant indicative value, which updates every few seconds; the public will see it free on a 15-minute delay. A Settled figure is recomputed later, when late data arrives, for research and records; if it shows a Daily Fixed figure was off, the correction is published openly and is never pushed into future figures.

The calculation

How one Life Unit is calculated, line by line

Read this as a worked example. The structure below is the real method. The prices in it are placeholders, chosen so the total matches today's working figure of about USD 1,750 a day in spending, until data-farming nodes collect real prices in each of the ten places. Each line says where its real number will come from. Tap any part to see its lines.

How it will become real: Light sets the quantities first (how much food, which home, which vehicle, how many trips), then the same quantities are priced in every place, the way official price indexes work. Spending, each part and tax are published separately. Light's USD 1,500 to 2,000 a day of spending is a sanity check, not a target: once real prices arrive, the total is whatever they add up to, and the balancing "Saving for the future" line is replaced by an amount Light sets.

One Life Unit = median-six average of the ten places' daily cost, where each place's daily cost = (home + food + vehicle + fuel and energy + travel + insurance + everything else) ÷ (1 − that place's tax rate)

Step 2: the ten places

Each place gets its own daily cost from its own prices and its own taxes. The two highest and two lowest daily costs are dropped and the middle six averaged, once, on the complete city totals, so one city's bubble or crash barely moves the unit. Today all ten carry the same placeholder figure, so the trim changes nothing yet: it will matter once real city costs arrive. Until then, ties are broken by place name (A to Z), only so that everyone sees the same table. The one-year change column comes from the proxy backtest: how each place's cost of living moved over the past year (real local home and consumer price indices, converted at real exchange rates).

The list is the Julius Baer Global Wealth and Lifestyle Report 2026 top ten, used as the starting point. The rule gives every inhabited continent a seat, with at most two places per country; this list still lacks the Americas and Africa, which the rule must correct.

Where the numbers come from

How the Life Unit's value is measured, and how fresh it is

Semi-real time, not live trading. The counters tick every tenth of a second, but they tick from fixed rates, not live market data. Coin prices come from a shared price feed that is refreshed by hand for now (about daily; the last refresh time is shown at the top). The cost of a Life Unit changes slowly because homes, insurance, food and taxes are re-priced monthly or quarterly. The official figure will be fixed once a day.

Where every number comes from (tap to open)

RealCoin prices: CoinGecko, a public market-data service that averages prices across many exchanges. Copied into this page's shared feed at each refresh; not a live trading price. The official standard will use hour-averaged prices weighted by real trading volume across many exchanges.

EstimateThe two history charts: a proxy backtest. Its inputs are real: home prices (Bank for International Settlements), consumer prices (World Bank), exchange rates (US Federal Reserve) and Bitcoin (Coinbase), all via the St. Louis Fed (FRED), quarterly since 2016. Its dollar level is scaled to the placeholder figure, and the trim rule was chosen on the same data.

PlaceholderToday's cost of a Life Unit: USD 2,692.31 on 8 October 2026 from the v0.3 abundant-day costs, which are working placeholders until real prices are collected in the ten places. Between data updates it rises at 2.6% a year, the average pace of the proxy backtest.

EstimatePopulation, debt and world output: UN, IIF and IMF figures, advanced at their published yearly rates.

EstimateThe basic-day floor: about USD 9.84 a day for healthy food, safe water, shelter, energy and care (from SOFI 2026, Rao and Min 2018, and Kikstra et al. 2021). It is a red line on the measuring stick, not something coins buy, and it still has to be calibrated to each region's purchasing power.

PlannedWhen the standard is live: independent data-farming nodes gather sale prices, insurance, fuel, food and tax data in the ten places for the quantities Light has fixed; verifier nodes recompute the figure; the middle six places are averaged; the official figure is fixed once a day over a one-hour window and recorded on a blockchain. Missing or stale data is never filled in silently: it is shown with its age. Businesses can receive an instant value every few seconds; the public sees it free on a short delay.

How often each part changes: coin prices, seconds (refreshed here about daily); fuel and travel, daily; food, weekly to monthly; homes, insurance and vehicles, monthly to quarterly; taxes, yearly.
